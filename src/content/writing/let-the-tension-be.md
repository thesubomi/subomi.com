I'm writing this on a train to Kraków. I haven't been to my favourite city in Poland in a while, and it was time to fix that.

I lived in Kraków for two years before I moved to Warsaw, and it still feels like the city that welcomed me properly. The Old Town, the walks along the river, the way the whole place moves a little slower than the capital. Warsaw is where I work. Kraków is where I breathe. If you ever get the chance to visit this beautiful country, tell the pilot to drop you off in Kraków first. Thank me later.

Trains are where I do my best thinking. No meetings, no notifications worth answering, just the fields going past and my phone in my hand. So somewhere between Warsaw and here, I started going through the pile of screenshots I'd collected while scrolling LinkedIn this week.

I screenshot a lot. Most of them sit in my camera roll and quietly die there. This one didn't. This is the one I chose to write about, because when I first saw it, I felt seen.

## The tension I've been carrying

For the longest time I've been battling with a question about how to lead. Do I show up as the stern, slightly cold leader who keeps everyone on their toes? Or the warm, cheerful one people actually enjoy being around?

This year I went full-time on Colorteam, and the team grew. The question got louder. When it was just me, I only had to manage my own moods. Now there are people looking to me for direction, for energy, and for the standard.

Both sides have a pull.

The stern version looks like control. Deadlines get met. Nobody takes the work lightly. But it comes at a cost. People stop telling you things. Ideas stay in heads instead of making it to the table. The work gets done, but nobody enjoys doing it, and you can feel that in the work.

The warm version looks like a great place to be. People relax. They laugh. They talk to you. But here's the honest part: too much of the warm and cheerful doesn't hold up over time. Things slip. Lines blur. Feedback gets softened until it stops being feedback. You do need to find a balance.

## Why this post landed

What this post did was put words to that balance. Being able to lock in and get things done, then switch a few minutes later and be the goofball in the room, is a rare thing.

I had been treating it as a choice. Pick a side and stick to it. Be consistent. But the post reframed it for me. The skill isn't choosing stern or warm. The skill is the switch. Knowing which one the moment needs, and being able to move between them without losing yourself.

Driven and fun. Hungry and humble. Focused and kind. None of those pairs cancel each other out. They make each other stronger. The focus earns you the right to play, and the play makes people want to give you their focus.

Reading that made sense of the tension I've been grappling with. It isn't a flaw I need to fix. It's the point.

## What I'm doing differently

From now on, I'm going to express myself more freely with my team. When it's time to work, we work. When it's time to play, we do exactly that.

In practice, that looks like a few things:

- **Being clear about the mode.** When we're working, the standard doesn't bend. Deadlines are deadlines. Feedback is direct.
- **Showing up as a person, not just a title.** Laughing at the joke in the group chat. Sharing what's going on with me, not only what's due.
- **Celebrating out loud.** Wins get named. Good work gets noticed by the whole team, not just by me.
- **Not apologising for either side.** The serious version of me and the goofy version are the same person. Both get to show up.

I've always been a chief campaigner of "work hard, play harder." I live it in my own life, on my runs and in my music. Bringing it to my team simply extends that thinking to the people I spend most of my time with. It would be strange to preach it everywhere except the place I spend most of my days.

## Let it

My wife has been playing with Mel Robbins' "Let Them" idea lately, and she can't stop raving about it. The idea, as she explains it to me, is to stop trying to control what other people do and let them do it. It has genuinely changed how she handles a lot of things.

My version is shorter: let it. Let the tension be.

I trained as an architect, and one of the first things you learn is that tension isn't the enemy. A suspension bridge stands because its cables are pulled tight. Take the tension away and the whole thing falls. The pull between the serious and the playful works the same way. It's what holds the structure up.

So I'm not going to try to resolve it anymore. It's normal, and you should welcome it. It's part of what makes me unique, and you too, if you feel the same way.

The train is pulling into Kraków now. Time to switch modes.
